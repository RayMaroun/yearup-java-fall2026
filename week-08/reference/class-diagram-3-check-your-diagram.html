Check Your Diagram

Part 3 of 3. Four steps from a drawn diagram to the green light. Walk one order through it, look for the usual traps, save it where it belongs, then show it.

What you need firstYour drawn diagram from part 2, open next to this page.
Be your own first reviewerEvery question here is one you can be asked. Answer it from the diagram, not from what you plan to code.
Pick your optionThe order to walk through uses the words of your capstone. Your option is the one you picked in part 1.
What you leave withA diagram you can explain box by box, pushed to your repository, and the green light to start coding.

Steps0 of 4

    1

    Walk one order through it

    finger on the diagram
    The order to walk throughOne in the middle size, with two premium toppings (one of them with an extra portion), one regular topping and one sauce. One medium drink. And .

    Do

    Put a finger on your diagram and follow this one order from the first screen to the receipt. Answer each question out loud. Tick it when the diagram answers it without you adding "well, in the code I would...".

    Done looks like

    You can tell the story of one order from start to finish using only what is drawn.

    Watch out

    • The place where you get stuck is the place to change in the diagram.
    • Try a second order with no main item at all. The text has a rule for it.
    2

    Check for the usual traps

    Six traps, and the question that gets you out

    1. The class that does everything. One box with twenty methods. Whose data does each method really use?
    2. A class for every choice. If two boxes differ only by a name or a number, is that a class or a value?
    3. The same thing in two places. If the two ever disagree, which one is right?
    4. Inheritance to borrow code. Say "A is a kind of B" out loud. Does it sound true?
    5. The parent with nothing in it. What do the children share that could live there?
    6. The method that cannot know. Where does it get what it needs: its own class, or its inputs?

    Done looks like

    You looked for each trap in your own diagram and either did not find it or fixed it.

    Watch out

    • A diagram can avoid all six and still be yours. Do not copy a classmate's to be safe: you will have to explain every box.
    3

    Save it and keep it current

    it lives in your repository

    Do

    1. Keep the .drawio file in your project. It is the one you can still change.
    2. File, Export as, PNG. Save the picture in your project too, and show it in your README.
    3. When the code changes a class, change the diagram the same day and export again. A diagram that no longer matches the code is worse than none.

    Done looks like

    The diagram file and its picture are committed and pushed, the picture is visible in the README, and both match what you are about to code.

    Watch out

    • Export the whole page, not a selection, and check that no box is cut off in the picture.
    4

    Show it to Raymond

    two short checks, then the green light

    Do

    1. Check 1: your classes, before any drawing. Bring your sorted words and your seven decisions from part 1. A list on paper or in a file is enough. Raymond calls you: you do not have to ask.
    2. Check 2: your diagram. Push the .drawio file to your repository before the deadline, finished or not, and bring it to the call.
    3. After each check you leave with a short list of what to change. Change it, push again, and say so.
    4. The times of both checks and the deadlines are on The Capstone 2 Path.

    Done looks like

    Raymond gave you the green light for your diagram. Only then do you start to code.

    Watch out

    • An unfinished diagram on time is better than a finished one too late: it can still be fixed.
    • You will be asked "why" about your boxes. A diagram you cannot explain does not get the green light, even if it is correct.

    If you only remember one thing

    Draw it, then walk an order through it. Every place where you cannot say who holds a piece of information, or who works something out, is a place to change. The diagram is finished when the order gets from the first screen to the receipt without you inventing anything on the way.
