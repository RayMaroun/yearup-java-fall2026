Class Diagram Guide

Ten steps from the capstone text to a class diagram you can explain. This page does not tell you which classes to draw. It shows you how to find them, how to draw them, and which questions to ask your own design before you write any code.

What the diagram isThe plan of your classes, before any code: what each one holds, what each one can do, and how they are joined.
There is no single right answerTwo good diagrams for the same shop can look different. A good one covers the whole text and lets you explain every box.
Two kinds of exampleSteps 1 and 3 work on your own capstone text. The drawing steps use a cinema on purpose: you see how to draw, and the classes stay yours to find.
Stuck for 20 minutes?Name the step and the question you cannot answer: "step 3, decision 5, my topping cannot know the size". A precise question is half the answer.

    Read the text with two colors

    nouns and verbs

    Do

    1. Read your capstone text once from start to finish. Then read it again with two colors: one for the things (nouns), one for the actions (verbs). The text itself gives you this hint.
    2. Read the price table the same way. Every row and every column is information your design has to hold somewhere.
    3. Write the words down as a plain list. No boxes yet.

    2

    Sort your words

    class, attribute, method or value

    A cinema sells tickets and snacks. Four questions for every word on a list

    1. Does it have data of its own and things it can answer? It is probably a class.
    2. Does it only describe one thing? It is an attribute of that thing.
    3. Is it one choice out of a fixed list (a size, a flavor, a kind)? It is a value of an attribute, not a class per choice.
    4. Is it something to do or to work out? It is a method. Decide later whose job it is.

    Try it on another shop: what is each cinema word?

    recliner
    size of the popcorn
    work out the total
    order
    flavor of the drink

    Done looks like

    Every word on your capstone list is sorted. You have a handful of classes, each with a few attributes, and a list of actions that still need an owner.

    Watch out

    • A long list of choices (every bread, every topping) is data, not a list of classes.
    • If two "classes" hold the same attributes and do the same things, ask what makes them different.
    3

    Make the seven decisions of this capstone

    before you draw anything

    Do

    Every diagram for this capstone answers the same seven questions, one way or another. Open each one, read what the text says, look at the ways to go, and choose. There is more than one good answer: what matters is that you choose on purpose and can say why. The names of your classes are yours.

    The text saysCustomers order , and can add drinks and .

    Ways to go, and what each one costs

    • Each product is its own kind of thing. Each one holds exactly what it needs: a drink has a flavor, a has a . More boxes, but every box is easy to read.
    • One general product with a field that says which kind it is. Fewer boxes. But a drink then carries a it never uses, and one price method has to handle every kind.

    Test your choiceRead the attributes of each product out loud. Does every attribute make sense for that product?

    The text saysA customer can place an order with 0 or more , plus drinks and .

    Ways to go, and what each one costs

    • One collection per kind of product. Quick to draw. But every question the order answers (what is in me, how much in total) has to be asked once per collection, and a new product changes the order in several places.
    • One collection for everything. The order asks every thing the same questions and never changes. For that, the products must have something in common. You decide what they share, and which of the tools from the last two weeks says so.

    Test your choiceThe shop adds a fourth product next month. Count the places in your order that change.

    The text saysThe customer can then choose their toppings.

    Ways to go, and what each one costs

    • A yes or no field for each topping. Easy to start. But a new topping changes the class, and two meats on one cannot be said.
    • A collection of toppings on the item. Any number, in any mix. Each topping is then a thing that goes into a collection. What does one topping have to hold?

    Test your choicePut two meats and a topping the shop invents next month on one . Does your design take them without a new field?

    The text saysToppings are categorized as regular and premium. In the price table, meats and cheeses have different prices, and regular toppings and sauces are included.

    Ways to go, and what each one costs

    • Different kinds of topping. Each kind works out its own price in its own way. More boxes. Ask what the kinds have in common, and where that lives.
    • One kind of topping that carries a mark for its group. One box. But one price method has to handle every group, and it grows with every rule.
    • In between: two kinds, with a mark inside one of them. Fewer boxes than the first way, shorter methods than the second. You decide where the line goes.

    Test your choicePick one meat, one cheese and one regular topping. What is different between them in your diagram? Where do 1.00 and .75 come from?

    The text saysCustomers can request extra toppings, but premium toppings come at an additional cost. The price table has one column per size.

    Ways to go, and what each one costs

    • The topping is told the size when it is asked for its price. The item keeps the size, the topping keeps its own facts, and nothing is stored twice.
    • The item works out the price of its own toppings. The item has the size and the toppings, so it can. Its price method becomes long, and the topping knows nothing about its own price.
    • Each topping remembers the size itself. It works. But the size now lives in two places, and they can disagree.

    Test your choiceExtra cheese on the middle size. Say who knows it is cheese, who knows it is extra, who knows the size, and how the three meet in one method.

    The text saysThe application displays the order details and the total cost, and saves each order to its own receipt file.

    Ways to go, and what each one costs

    • Each thing answers for its own price, the order adds the answers. Every box does one small job. To change a price you know where to look.
    • One place works everything out. One long method that has to know every product, every topping and every rule. Each change in the shop is a change there.

    Test your choicePut a finger on the method that prices the main item, then the drink, then the side, then the total. Then on the one place that writes the receipt file. A class or a method are both fine for the receipt, as long as it is one place.

    The text saysEach can . Sauces and sides are included. If an order has 0 , the customer must purchase or a drink.

    • The special option. A yes or no choice for one .
    • Sauces and included sides. They cost nothing, but the receipt has to show them. Are they toppings like the others, or something else?
    • The rule for an order with no main item. A rule is something to check. Which class is able to check it?

    Done looks like

    Seven ticks, and for each one a sentence you could say out loud: "I went this way because...".

    Watch out

    • The shortest way to draw is rarely the shortest way to code. Read what each way costs later.
    • If a test makes you change one decision, check the others again: they depend on each other.
    4

    Draw one box

    Class Diagram Reference

    Do

    1. One box per class, three parts: the name, the attributes, the methods.
    2. Every attribute is written - name: Type. Every method is written + name(input: Type): ReturnType.
    3. Give each action from your list to the class that holds the data it needs. If a class has to ask three others for everything, the method is in the wrong box.
    4. Leave out plain getters and setters. They hide the methods that matter.

    A drawn box: press a band

    Three bands, always in this orderPress the name, the attributes or the methods.

    Try it: which lines are complete? Press each one

    Read each line as a promiseA complete line tells the reader everything without opening the code.

    Done looks like

    Every attribute has a type. Every method shows what goes in and what comes out. Each box could be handed to a classmate to code without a single question.

    Watch out

    • Prices are money. Text cannot be added up, and a whole number cannot hold 1.50.
    • Where there can be several of something, one field is not enough.
    5

    Connect the boxes

    Class Diagram ReferenceClass Diagram Reference, week 7

    Three sentences, three kinds of line

    1. "A is a kind of B." Inheritance: a solid line with a hollow triangle that touches B, the parent.
    2. "A has B" or "A holds several B." A line from A to B with a hollow diamond on A, the owner.
    3. "A can do what B describes." A implements the interface B: a dashed line with a hollow triangle that touches B.

    Try it: say the sentence, see the line

    Say it out loudPress a sentence.

    Done looks like

    Every box is joined to at least one other. For every line you can say its sentence out loud and it sounds true. Abstract classes and interfaces are marked.

    Watch out

    • The triangle touches the parent, never the child.
    • In diagrams.net, drag each end of a line onto a box until the box turns green. A line that only sits near a box is attached to nothing.
    • Inheritance is for "is a kind of", not for borrowing a few lines of code.
    6

    Build the picture in five passes

    Drawing it in diagrams.netA whole design on one page

    Do, in diagrams.net

    1. Names. Type class in the shapes search and drag one Class shape per class onto the page. Type only the names. Leave room around every box.
    2. Attributes. Double-click the middle band, one attribute per line.
    3. Methods. Double-click the bottom band, one method per line.
    4. Lines. Hover over a box and drag one of its blue arrows onto the other box. Then click the line and set its ends in the Format panel, Style tab: hollow triangle, diamond, or dashed.
    5. Marks. The word «interface» above an interface name, italics for an abstract class, a star where one box holds several of another.

    Watch a cinema diagram grow: one pass per press

    Pass 1 of 5: namesOne box per class, with room around each. Nothing inside yet.

    Same diagram, two layouts: press to switch

    Hard to readEvery box and every line is correct, but the interface sits at the bottom, the child is far from its parent, and the lines run in every direction. A reader has to untangle it first.

    Done looks like

    Every class of your list is a box with three bands, every line is attached at both ends, and the picture reads from top to bottom without crossing lines.

    Watch out

    • Do not start with the lines. Lines between half-empty boxes get redrawn three times.
    • Parents and interfaces go above, children below. The class that holds others goes to the left or on top.

    Walk one order through it

    finger on the diagram
    The order to walk throughOne main item in the middle size, with two premium toppings (one of them extra), one regular topping and one sauce. One medium drink. One side product.

    Do

    Put a finger on your diagram and follow this one order from the first screen to the receipt. Answer each question out loud. Tick it when the diagram answers it without you adding "well, in the code I would...".

    Done looks like

    You can tell the story of one order from start to finish using only what is drawn.

    Watch out

    • The place where you get stuck is the place to change in the diagram.
    • Try a second order with no main item at all. The text has a rule for it.
    9

    Check for the usual traps

    Six traps, and the question that gets you out

    1. The class that does everything. One box with twenty methods. Whose data does each method really use?
    2. A class for every choice. If two boxes differ only by a name or a number, is that a class or a value?
    3. The same thing in two places. If the two ever disagree, which one is right?
    4. Inheritance to borrow code. Say "A is a kind of B" out loud. Does it sound true?
    5. The parent with nothing in it. What do the children share that could live there?
    6. The method that cannot know. Where does it get what it needs: its own class, or its inputs?

    Done looks like

    You looked for each trap in your own diagram and either did not find it or fixed it.

    Watch out

    • A diagram can avoid all six and still be yours. Do not copy a classmate's to be safe: you will have to explain every box.
    10

    Save it and keep it current

    it lives in your repository

    Do

    1. Keep the .drawio file. It is the one you can still change.
    2. File, Export as, PNG. Save the picture in your repository and show it in your README.
    3. When the code changes a class, change the diagram the same day and export again. A diagram that no longer matches the code is worse than none.

    Done looks like

    The picture is committed in your repository, visible in the README, and matches what you are about to code.

    Watch out

    • Export the whole page, not a selection, and check that no box is cut off in the picture.

    If you only remember one thing

    Draw it, then walk an order through it. Every place where you cannot say who holds a piece of information, or who works something out, is a place to change. The diagram is finished when the order gets from the first screen to the receipt without you inventing anything on the way.
